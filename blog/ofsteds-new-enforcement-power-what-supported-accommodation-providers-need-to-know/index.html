Ofsted’s New Enforcement Power: What Supported Accommodation Providers Need to Know

Ofsted updated its social care enforcement policy on 20 August 2026. Find out what the new fining power means for Supported Accommodation providers.

27 August 2026

On 20 August 2026, Ofsted updated its Social Care Enforcement Policy to reflect new legislative powers. For Supported Accommodation providers, the most important change is a new power to impose a monetary penalty on people who commit the offence of carrying on or managing an unregistered social care service.

The change follows the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, which received Royal Assent on 29 April 2026. Supported Accommodation had already been subject to mandatory Ofsted registration, and operating without the appropriate registration was already an offence. What is new is the additional enforcement route: Ofsted can now fine an operator rather than relying only on prosecution where the legal threshold is met.

What has actually changed?

The 20 August 2026 update is important because it expands Ofsted’s enforcement toolkit. The underlying registration duty is not new. A person who carries on or manages Supported Accommodation for looked-after children or care leavers aged 16 and 17 without being registered commits an offence under the Care Standards Act 2000.

The updated policy says Ofsted may prosecute and now also has power under the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 to impose a fine on someone who commits that offence. The policy was also updated to reflect a separate change allowing certain notices to social care providers and managers to be served by email.

Why this matters for Supported Accommodation

For new operators, the practical lesson is straightforward: regulatory status should be established before the service starts accommodating eligible children. An application being prepared, or even being in progress, does not automatically mean a provider is free to operate.

Ofsted’s policy also confirms that it can visit a service it reasonably believes is operating without registration. Inspectors may examine the condition and management of premises, interview relevant people and children who consent, and inspect or copy documents. Where safeguarding concerns arise, referrals may also be made to the local authority and police.

Registration is only the first stage

The same enforcement policy sets out the range of actions Ofsted can use against registered services where regulations are not being met. These can include requirements, compliance notices, monitoring activity, restrictions on accommodation, suspension and cancellation. This is why compliance should be treated as an ongoing operating discipline rather than a one-off registration exercise.

What providers should check now

  • Confirm that every service requiring registration is correctly registered before operating.
  • Review whether any changes to your service model, premises or management arrangements affect registration requirements.
  • Make sure leaders understand Ofsted’s current enforcement policy and the difference between recommendations, requirements, compliance notices and other enforcement actions.
  • Keep evidence of compliance organised and retrievable rather than waiting for inspection or regulatory contact.

The wider direction is becoming clearer: Supported Accommodation is now an established regulated sector, and Ofsted has a broader set of tools available where providers operate outside the registration framework or fail to meet legal requirements.
