Closed Cultures in Supported Accommodation: What History Teaches Providers

By Taps Kundoro, Supported Accommodation Providers Consultancy · Last reviewed 28 October 2026

Supported accommodation should help young people build confidence, maintain meaningful relationships and develop the skills they need for adulthood. Yet a service can provide suitable housing and have detailed policies while still developing a culture in which young people feel unheard, staff avoid challenging poor practice and organisational reputation takes priority over wellbeing.

This article focuses on supported accommodation for young people in England, particularly looked-after children and care leavers aged 16 and 17. Understanding closed cultures requires attention to both the history of institutional failures and the personal experiences young people bring into accommodation.

Understanding closed culture

The Care Quality Commission describes a closed culture as a poor culture that can lead to harm, including abuse and breaches of human rights. Its warning signs include punitive attitudes, weak leadership, restrictions without adequate justification and defensive responses to concerns. Although CQC guidance concerns services within its own remit, these warning signs provide a useful lens for examining supported accommodation. Ofsted regulates the supported accommodation considered here.

Source: CQC guidance on closed cultures

A closed culture does not depend on locked doors or an isolated building. It can develop through everyday relationships: whose account is believed, who makes decisions and what happens when someone disagrees. A young person may be free to leave the premises but still feel unable to question staff because they fear losing their accommodation or being labelled difficult.

The historical imbalance of power

Historical failures across institutions show the danger of allowing professional authority to outweigh children's experiences. In its 2022 final report, the Independent Inquiry into Child Sexual Abuse identified repeated failures of leadership and child protection. Institutions sometimes protected their reputations, failed to report allegations and kept inadequate records. Having policies did not ensure that those policies were followed.

Source: IICSA executive summary

The relevance to supported accommodation is the pattern of institutional defensiveness. When a complaint is treated primarily as a threat to the provider, attention can shift away from what happened to the young person. Managers may focus on explaining an incident rather than investigating it. Staff may learn that loyalty means protecting colleagues.

The practical lesson is that a young person's account deserves careful attention even when it challenges an established member of staff. Fair investigation requires evidence and an open mind. Neither a professional title nor a young person's previous difficulties should determine credibility in advance.

The legacy of blaming young people

The failures exposed in Rotherham provide another historical warning. The government's response to the Jay and Casey reports explicitly recognised a culture of inaction and denial in which victims were dismissed and ignored. It also identified the need for stronger accountability and better information sharing.

Source: Government response to child sexual exploitation failures

For supported accommodation, this history should prompt scrutiny of everyday language. Describing someone simply as “attention seeking”, “manipulative” or “making poor choices” can close down curiosity about distress, coercion or unmet needs.

Young people remain responsible participants in decisions about their lives, but independence should not become a reason to withdraw protection. A missed appointment, an unexplained absence or an angry response should lead staff to explore what happened and what support is needed. Recording behaviour accurately is more useful than attaching a judgement to someone's character. Our recording templates for supported accommodation are built around factual, impact-focused records.

The history of regulation

England introduced mandatory registration and quality standards for supported accommodation for looked-after children and care leavers aged 16 and 17 in October 2023. This marked an important change in oversight of provision previously described as unregulated. “Unregulated” did not mean that local authorities had no responsibilities, but these services had not been subject to this specific registration framework.

Source: Department for Education announcement

Regulation provides an external safeguard, but organisational culture also depends on daily practice. A service can prepare thoroughly for inspection while overlooking how young people experience an ordinary evening, a disagreement or a request for help. For a summary of what the rules require, see the Supported Accommodation Regulations 2023 explained.

Ofsted's registration guidance emphasises that supported accommodation should not be the default option for every 16- or 17-year-old. Suitability depends on the individual child's needs. Providers should therefore be willing to acknowledge when a placement cannot offer the right support.

Source: Ofsted registration policy

The personal histories young people bring

Historical issues also exist at an individual level. A young person may arrive after bereavement, abuse, disrupted placements, discrimination or repeated experiences of adults failing to keep promises. Staff should explore these experiences sensitively rather than assume that every young person has the same history.

For someone who has previously been ignored, making a complaint may feel risky. Someone who has experienced repeated moves may interpret disagreement as a threat to their home. Silence or apparent agreement should therefore not automatically be taken as evidence that a young person feels safe.

Understanding these experiences requires consistent relationships, clear explanations and time. Staff should explain decisions, follow through on commitments and acknowledge mistakes.

Keeping the service open

An open culture gives young people meaningful influence over their support. It also maintains connections beyond the provider, including safe family relationships, friendships, advocates, education and community networks.

The Supported Accommodation Regulations include requirements concerning safe relationships, accessible information about complaints and advocacy, and reviews of support quality that take account of children's views. These requirements give providers practical opportunities to hear experiences that routine monitoring may miss.

Source: Supported Accommodation Regulations

Leaders should ask what changed following feedback, whether young people can speak privately to someone outside the service and whether staff feel able to question a manager's decision. Very few complaints may reflect satisfaction, but leaders should also consider whether the process feels accessible and safe. Independent monthly monitoring visits are one way to give young people and staff someone outside the service to talk to.

History shows how harm can persist when authority goes unquestioned and concerns are explained away. In supported accommodation, preventing a closed culture means making challenge possible in ordinary daily life, and demonstrating, through action, that speaking up leads to listening, fair scrutiny and improvement.

Frequently asked questions

A poor culture that can lead to harm, where young people feel unheard, staff avoid challenging poor practice and the organisation's reputation comes before wellbeing. It can develop in an open building through everyday relationships and decisions.

The CQC lists punitive attitudes, weak leadership, restrictions without adequate justification and defensive responses to concerns. In supported accommodation, labels such as "attention seeking" or "manipulative" and very few complaints can also be signs worth checking.

Give young people real influence over their support, keep links with safe family, friends, advocates, education and the community, make complaints and advocacy easy to reach, and show that feedback leads to change.
